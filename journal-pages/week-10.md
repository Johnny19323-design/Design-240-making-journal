## In-Class Activities
### 1. Progress Reports and Padlet Critique

Week 10 was a major turning point in the project. According to the class overview and slides, the purpose of this week was to present a progress report, receive structured feedback through Padlet, review other groups’ boards through a gallery walk, and then turn that critique into a concrete action plan. The class used a “gamified critique” format with defined roles, time pressure, likes, and cross-group viewing, which made feedback more structured and visible. The Padlet system organised comments into four columns: Technical Feedback, Chair’s Summary, Conceptual Feedback, and Critical Thinking. This meant that the critique was not random or informal. It pushed each project to be examined from different perspectives, including feasibility, conceptual clarity, provocation, and future development.

![Class Image](../assets/week-10/design240(p4).png)
*Group Padlet board used during the Week 10 critique session*

In my progress report, I presented the project as no longer simply a map of DOC campsites, but as a more critical investigation into how official environmental data structures public understanding of natural space. The most important shift in the project at this stage was a clearer statement of direction: using DOC campsite data to question how nature is categorised and made legible through data systems. This marked a significant development from earlier weeks. Before this point, the project was still close to becoming another campsite browsing tool. By Week 10, I was much more committed to using the dataset critically, asking not only where campsites are, but how official data defines access, facilities, and public nature itself.

The feedback I received through Padlet strongly reinforced this shift. Across the Technical Feedback column, several comments noted that moving away from showing all campsites and instead building a smaller curated route database made the project far more feasible. It reduced technical complexity and gave the interface a clearer structure for route ranking, route comparison, and recommendation logic. In other words, the feedback supported the idea that a route-based system was a stronger technical direction than a general campsite map.

The Conceptual Feedback and Critical Thinking comments were even more influential. A recurring idea was that the most compelling part of the project was no longer the visualisation of locations, but the comparison between the official data view and the lived or experiential view of place. This was important because it reframed the project’s purpose. Instead of only helping users find campsites, the project could encourage them to reflect on how public nature is defined, organised, and experienced differently through data systems. Another key comment suggested that recommendation itself should be made more transparent, so users can understand that route scores are not neutral facts but forms of design judgement based on values such as access, duration, facilities, and activity consistency. This helped me realise that the recommendation system was not just a functional device. It was also part of the project’s argument.

![Class Image](../assets/week-10/design240(p3).png)
*Structured Padlet feedback across Technical, Chair’s Summary, Conceptual, and Critical Thinking categories*

The Chair’s Summary comments were also useful because they distilled the broader discussion into a few direct points. The feedback made clear that the project became stronger when it stopped asking “where are campsites?” and started asking how official data shapes public understanding of nature. It also highlighted that building my own recommendation logic from the dataset, rather than copying public ratings or existing platform reviews, gave the project a clearer design position. This meant the project was beginning to move away from reproducing an existing tourism interface and toward becoming a curated critical response.

### 2. Gallery Walk

Another important in-class activity was the gallery walk. The slides instructed us to visit other groups’ Padlet boards, read their Technical and Conceptual feedback, add our own comments, and use the like button to highlight the most useful advice. This extended the critique beyond the immediate presentation group and exposed each project to a wider range of perspectives. The class format therefore encouraged critique to operate as a shared system rather than a closed small-group discussion.

For me, the gallery walk was helpful because it made my own project feel part of a wider field of approaches. Seeing how other students’ projects were discussed helped me better understand how critique works when it is structured around data choice, provocation, feasibility, and intended public impact. It also reinforced the importance of being able to explain not only what the prototype does, but why its design decisions matter.

### 3. Action Plan

The final in-class activity was to translate the feedback into an action plan. The weekly overview required a short reflective summary of the most significant feedback, followed by clear and concrete development points. This was especially useful because the project had now reached a stage where feedback needed to become decisions rather than just observations.

From the critique, I identified three major actions. First, I needed to stop thinking of the project as a general campsite map and commit fully to a smaller curated route-based system. Second, I needed to strengthen the conceptual distinction between “officially organised nature” and “experienced nature,” so that the interface would not function only as a practical planning tool. Third, I needed to make the recommendation logic of the system legible, showing that route ranking is based on chosen criteria rather than presenting it as neutral fact. These actions became the foundation for the next stage of development.

## Independent Study
### 1. Project Development

The independent study for Week 10 focused entirely on project development, guided by the critique and the action plan. This matched the course overview, which stated that this week’s independent study should be fully dedicated to applying feedback and moving the project forward in preparation for the Week 12 showcase.

In response to the feedback, I made a clear decision to refine the project into a curated route-selection system instead of a general campsite visualisation. Rather than trying to show every campsite in the DOC dataset, I began selecting a smaller subset of data and building my own route database. This new structure allowed the project to offer different route types, such as camping routes, hiking routes, and water-based routes, and to support more focused user decisions. This shift was both conceptual and technical. Technically, it made the project more manageable. Conceptually, it made the work more distinct, because it was no longer trying to replicate an official campsite map.

![Class Image](../assets/week-10/design240(p1).png)
*Earlier prototype showing a broader nationwide campsite map with many points and a more general browsing logic*

At this stage, I also decided that the recommendation logic should come from my own reading and evaluation of the data, rather than relying on external ratings or platform reviews. This was an important design decision. It meant that the recommendation system itself could become part of the project’s critical framework. Routes could be assessed through criteria such as route duration, access type, activity consistency, facilities, and suitability for different users. For example, a shorter route might be more suitable for weekend travel, while a route with stronger consistency in activity type could be easier for users to understand and compare. In this way, the project became not only a selection tool, but also a demonstration of how data can be interpreted and judged.

The screenshots from this stage of development show that the prototype was already beginning to move in this new direction. Earlier versions still displayed many points across the country and retained a broader campsite-map logic. Later versions began to introduce more selective filtering, route-group structures, and a clearer division between route information and campsite detail. This transition is important because it shows that the Week 10 feedback was not only noted in writing but directly applied to the interface.

### 2. Reflection on Direction Change

The most important outcome of Week 10 was not a single interface feature, but the clarification of the project’s core proposition. Up to this point, the project had gone through several iterations of map, filter, and campsite browsing. However, the feedback helped me recognise that the real value of the work was not in making another campsite tool, but in using official data to question how natural space is made visible, sortable, and understandable.

This is why the new direction became central: using DOC campsite data to question how nature is categorised and made legible through data systems. The route-based recommendation system gave me a more focused and usable way to develop that question. It allowed the project to remain interactive and practical, but also to show that route selection, ranking, and recommendation are never neutral. They are shaped by categories, values, and systems of organisation. That tension between usability and critique became the most important conceptual development of the week.

## Reflection

Week 10 was one of the most important weeks in the whole project because it forced me to make a real decision about what the project was becoming. Earlier weeks had raised many useful questions about uniqueness, form, and critical purpose, but Week 10 was the point where those questions turned into a clearer direction. The Padlet feedback made it obvious that simply building another campsite map would not be enough. The project needed to distinguish itself by asking how data structures public understanding of nature.

The most useful thing about this week was that feedback was organised in a way that made patterns visible. Technical comments showed that a curated route database was more feasible and manageable. Conceptual comments showed that the difference between official and experienced views of place was the most interesting part of the work. Critical comments showed that recommendation logic should be exposed as a design judgement rather than hidden as a neutral system. Taken together, these comments did not just improve the interface. They changed the project’s position.

As a result, Week 10 became the point where my project shifted from a campsite visualisation into a more focused route-selection system with a critical data perspective. This was not the final outcome yet, but it was the clearest step toward it.
