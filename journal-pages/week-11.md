# Week 11

## In-Class Activities
### 1. Journal Review

Week 11 is a consolidation week. Following the initial phases of exploration, critique and course correction, the focus has now shifted to reviewing and refining the work, and making all necessary preparations for submission and public presentation. According to the slides for Week 11, one of the key classroom tasks is the log review. We were asked to work in pairs, using the ‘Log Review Checklist’ to review our partner’s log entries from Weeks 6 to 10, focusing on the clarity and completeness of the entries, the balance between text and images, and technical aspects—such as whether the file structure is correct, whether Markdown formatting is consistent, whether images are organised appropriately, and whether the website displays correctly online. The slides also emphasised that an excellent process documentation submission should include all six journal entries, demonstrate an effective balance between text and visuals, and clearly reflect in-depth exploration of the process, conceptual development, and peer critique.

![Class Image](../assets/week-11/design240(p1).jpg)
*Journal Checklist used in Week 11 to review the clarity, completeness, and technical organisation of the process documentation*

This activity was especially useful because it made me step back from the content of the project and look at how the work was being communicated as a journal. Up to this point, most of my attention had gone into developing the artefact itself, responding to feedback, and refining the project direction. The checklist reminded me that process documentation is not only a record of what I did, but also an assessed design output in its own right. It needs to be clear, organised, and technically well presented. Reviewing another student’s journal through the checklist also made me more aware of the importance of consistency, especially in the relationship between text, images, formatting, and navigation.

After this paired review, I re-read my own entries from Weeks 6 to 10 and identified three key moments that had shaped the project direction. The first was the early proposal stage, when I realised I wanted to work with the DOC Campsites dataset not just as information, but as a way of examining how environmental space is classified. The second was the critique in Weeks 8 to 10, when repeated feedback made it clear that simply making another campsite map would not be enough. The third was the shift toward a smaller curated route database, which transformed the project from a broad campsite visualisation into a more focused system with its own recommendation logic. This self-review helped me prepare for the next activity, the studio consultation practice, because it forced me to organise the project journey into a clearer narrative.

### 2. Practice Consultations

The second major classroom activity was practice consultations. The slides framed this as rehearsal for the formal Week 12 studio consultation, which would assess not only how well the artefact worked, but also how clearly the student could discuss the project’s theme, data source, design journey, critical position, and intended impact. The rubric shown in the slides described a strong consultation as one with purposeful engagement, highly focused and cohesive articulation of ideas, pertinent reflection on the project and artefact, and strong critical understanding of the work’s position in relation to existing ideas. The prompt questions included: What is the project’s theme? What is the data source? What was a key moment in the design journey? How does the work challenge conventional ideas? What impact is intended? And what was most surprising in the making process?

This was a very useful exercise because it exposed whether I could actually explain the project clearly without relying on slides or written text. In previous weeks, I had already refined the direction of the work into a more focused proposition: using DOC campsite data to question how nature is categorised and made legible through data systems. However, saying this in one sentence is very different from sustaining a coherent discussion around it. Through the practice consultation, I became more aware of which parts of the project I could explain easily and which parts still felt less resolved. For example, I could clearly explain the shift from a general campsite map to a curated route recommendation system, and why that made the project more distinct. However, it was more difficult to explain succinctly how the project’s critical message related to broader ideas about data, categorisation, and public nature without sounding too general.

The self-reflection prompts on the slides were particularly helpful. They asked whether my answers consistently hit the mark, whether I maintained a clear narrative logic across different questions, and whether I was able to situate this work within a broader framework of data presentation. This made me realise that what I need to improve most at the moment is coherence. I must ensure that every answer returns to the same core proposition, rather than straying from the subject and getting bogged down in too many trivial details about the prototype. In this sense, this mock consultation is not only a rehearsal before the assessment, but also another way of clarifying the project’s knowledge architecture.

### 3. Showcase Planning

The third classroom activity was showcase planning. The slides asked us to think practically about what our work needed in order to be displayed effectively in Week 12: whether it required a screen, laptop, or power supply, whether extension cords or adapters were needed, whether the work would sit on a table or be wall-mounted, and whether anything needed to be printed such as the project statement. We were also asked to add ourselves to the showcase Miro board and coordinate our preferred installation location with the rest of the class. The slides made it clear that students needed to be ready to install their work at 2 pm on Friday 05 June.

This planning activity was important because it pushed the project beyond the screen and into the context of public display. Earlier in the semester, I had often been thinking about the project mainly as a prototype viewed on my own laptop. By Week 11, I had to think about it as an artefact that others would encounter in a shared physical space. This made issues of presentation more urgent. The work needed to be not only conceptually coherent and technically functioning, but also appropriate for exhibition. The showcase planning therefore connected the final design decisions to their public context, which was especially relevant because the grading rubric for the final artefact explicitly stated that high-level work should be carefully considered for public display.

## Independent Study    
### 1. Project Finalisation

For me, this stage was about refinement rather than major redirection. By Week 11, the core direction of the project had already been established through earlier critiques: the work would take the form of a web-based interactive project using a smaller curated route database, and its critical focus would be on how official environmental data organises public nature. The task now was to make sure all parts of the submission clearly reflected that direction. This meant continuing to refine the route-based prototype, making the interface more coherent, ensuring that filters and route logic were readable, and improving how route information and campsite details were displayed. It also meant revisiting the recommendation system so that it felt intentional and structured, rather than arbitrary.

At the same time, I had to finalise the journal itself. The Journal Checklist was very helpful here because it made the expectations concrete. It reminded me that the journal needed to cover all weeks clearly, balance visual and written evidence, and function properly as a GitHub Pages site. This pushed me to think not just about adding more content, but about presenting the process more effectively. I needed the journal to show not only the existence of experiments and feedback, but the logic of how the project had evolved.

### 2. Project Statement Finalisation

Another major part of the independent study was finalising the project statement. The slides stressed that, for a strong result, the project statement should be concise, comprehensive, and provide an excellent articulation of the work’s critical engagement and intended impact.

This made me re-read the statement carefully and check whether it was truly aligned with the artefact. By this stage, the project statement needed to express three things clearly: what the project is, what data it uses, and why it matters. Because my project had shifted significantly from the original campsite map idea, I needed the statement to reflect that later direction rather than describing earlier stages of the process. In particular, it had to communicate that the project is not simply a campsite-planning tool, but a critical route-selection system that reveals how natural spaces are made legible through categories such as access, facilities, activities, and recommendation logic. Finalising the statement was therefore another act of clarification, helping ensure that the public-facing explanation matched the conceptual development of the prototype.

### 3. Preparing for Submission and Consultation

The final area of independent study was preparing for both submission and the Week 12 studio consultation. These two tasks were closely connected. The slides made clear that the consultation would assess not only the artefact but also the ability to discuss the project’s development, learning, and critical positioning in relation to broader data ideas.

This meant I had to practise thinking about the project in two registers at once. On the one hand, I needed to ensure that all files, pages, images, and links were complete and working properly for submission. On the other hand, I needed to be able to talk clearly about why the project matters and what kind of insight it offers into data systems and public nature. This double preparation made Week 11 feel less like an “ending” week and more like a week of alignment, where all parts of the project had to be brought into agreement before the final presentation and assessment.

## Reflection

Week 11 was significant because it shifted the project into its final form. Earlier weeks were about exploration, critique, and major conceptual change. By contrast, Week 11 was about checking whether all parts of the work were now consistent: the journal, the artefact, the project statement, and my ability to speak about the project in consultation. The checklists and rubrics used in class were especially helpful because they made the assessment criteria visible. They showed that strong work is not only technically finished, but also coherent, critically engaged, and carefully presented for a public audience.

The most valuable lesson I’ve learnt this week is that the final stages of a project are not merely about polishing; they are about ensuring that all parts work together harmoniously. By the end of Week 11, I was able to assess more clearly whether my journal entries reflected the content of the project as presented, whether the project statement articulated perspectives consistent with the concepts embodied in the interface, and whether I could coherently explain this connection during discussions. In this sense, Week 11 marked a pivotal moment in the project’s transition from a collection of development stages to a complete final submission.
