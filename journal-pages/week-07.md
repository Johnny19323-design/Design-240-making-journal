# Week 07

## In-Class Activities

### 1. Concept Sketches

Week 07 focused on turning the project from a proposal and planning phase into a more active process of development through making. According to the weekly overview and class slides, the main in-class tasks were concept sketches, a making sprint, and “what if” variations. The learning outcomes for the week emphasised using sketching and peer feedback to evolve ideas, advancing the project through rapid prototyping, and exploring alternative directions through experimentation.

![Class Image](../assets/week-07/Design240(p2).jpg)

For the first activity, I brought my concept sketch of the DOC Campsites visualisation and displayed it for peer feedback. The sketch showed the project as a web-based interactive interface with a New Zealand map at the centre, campsite markers distributed geographically, filter controls on the left, and a campsite information panel on the right. I also annotated the sketch to explain the visual logic: colour would show campsite category, size would indicate capacity, and filters would support comparison across region, access method, facilities, and dog policy. More importantly, I wrote that the visualisation should not simply function as a tourism tool, but should highlight how public nature is organised through data. This made the intended critical position of the project visible at the sketch stage.

![Class Image](../assets/week-07/Design240(p1).png)

The students’ feedback was particularly helpful. Several students praised the level of detail in the drawings and noted that the design was already quite mature. At the same time, the questions they raised highlighted some uncertainties in the concept. One asked whether the final piece would be two-dimensional or three-dimensional, whilst another enquired whether similar systems already existed and what made my version unique. One comment asked what I hoped the audience would reflect on, which prompted me to think more clearly about the conceptual outcomes of the project, rather than just the interface itself. Another suggested that presenting the project as a physical object might work well, opening up an alternative direction I had not previously considered seriously. These comments align closely with the course’s emphasis on using peer feedback as a tool for refining conceptual sketches.

Having read through these comments, I realised that whilst my concept sketches were visually clear, they were still too similar in concept to existing campsite information tools. The most prominent issue raised in the feedback was not one of aesthetics, but rather of functional positioning and uniqueness. This made me even more aware that I needed to clarify what added value this project could offer beyond what is available on official map interfaces. In this sense, the value of the concept sketch exercise lies not only in visualising my ideas, but also in revealing the gap between a functional interface and a more compelling design solution.

### 2. Making Sprint

The second in-class activity was a 45-minute making sprint. The class slides framed this as a form of rapid prototyping: time-boxed, focused, and experimental, with the goal of creating something tangible and testable rather than polished. The slides also emphasised that the task should build directly from the skills roadmap developed in Week 06.

During this sprint, I focused on conducting my first visualisation experiments based on the dataset and the visualisation concepts I had previously devised. My primary objective at this stage was to test the concept of a map-based interface and to begin considering how to translate the campsite data into visual markers, filters and panels. Although I am still in the conceptual and visual exploration phase and have not yet produced a final interface, this sprint has helped me to start focusing the project on practical decision-making. I view the sketches as a provisional framework to be tested and revised, rather than a final design.

This sprint was significant because it moved the project from the annotation and planning stages to the first physical testing. Although the results are still somewhat rough, they helped me confirm that a map-based interface could serve as a strong starting point. At the same time, this reaffirmed the concerns raised in peer feedback: if the project remains solely at the level of browsing campsites, its critical depth and uniqueness remain insufficient.

### 3. “What if” Variations

The third in-class activity involved sharing the result of the making sprint with a partner and receiving three “what if” variations. The purpose of this exercise was to encourage speculative and alternative thinking, rather than simply refining the original idea. The class slides suggested examples such as changing scale, inverting the data, or shifting from a screen-based form to a physical one.

This exercise was closely linked to the feedback I had previously received on my sketches. The question of whether the project should remain in digital form or be transformed into a physical object began to take on increasing importance. The suggestion that ‘perhaps making it physical might be a good option’ prompted me to look beyond the laptop screen interface of my initial design and think more deeply about the project. Although I did not immediately abandon the web-based approach, this exercise broadened my understanding of the project’s future possibilities. It also made me realise that the key question this week was not merely ‘how should the data be presented?’, but rather ‘what form would most effectively convey the classification and management of environmental spaces?’

As the class session drew to a close, I felt that the most significant takeaway from this week was a shift in perspective. My initial sketches were merely a relatively simple interactive map, but through peer review and the exploration of hypothetical variations, I gradually realised that this project required a clearer purpose, particularly in terms of distinguishing it from existing information tools for the campsite.

## Independent Study
###  1. Project Development and Skill Building

The first independent study task for Week 07 was to continue developing the project and building technical skills directly from the outcomes of the making sprint and the “what if” variations. The weekly overview specifically asked us to document our learning process, including what we tried, what we learned, and how this moved the project forward.

In this phase, I continued thinking through the implications of the feedback I had received. The central issue was that although the concept sketch was detailed and visually convincing, it still risked functioning as a “normal tourism tool.” This made me more aware that my development work needed to move in two directions at once. On one hand, I still needed to build technical understanding of how a map-based interactive visualisation might work. On the other hand, I needed to think more critically about what the project would reveal beyond surface-level information.

This independent study therefore became less about polishing the sketch and more about clarifying the conceptual purpose of the prototype. I kept returning to the question of how the visualisation could reveal classification systems, data mediation, and environmental governance, rather than only presenting campsites as destinations. In this way, technical experimentation and conceptual refinement were happening at the same time.

### 2. Progress Report Preparation

The second independent study task was to prepare a short five-minute progress report for the following week’s class. The course required the slideshow to cover the current state of the project, key developments and decisions so far, visual research or references, and two or three questions for feedback.

Preparing for this progress report forced me to summarise the project more clearly. I had to explain where the project stood, what had changed since the Reflective Proposal and Proposal Consultation, and what open questions still remained. The progress report preparation was useful because it made me identify what the most important unresolved issues actually were. At this point, the most important questions were whether the project should remain screen-based, how it could become more distinct from existing campsite tools, and how to make the audience reflect on systems of classification rather than only consuming practical information.

This preparation also helped me recognise that documenting the project was not separate from developing it. The act of selecting images, sketches, and key points for the report became another way of reflecting on what had actually changed in my thinking.

## Reflection

Week 07 was valuable because it brought feedback directly into the centre of the design process. The concept sketch exercise showed me that a project can appear visually resolved while still containing major conceptual questions. The peer post-it comments were especially important because they did not only praise the work; they highlighted uncertainty around uniqueness, audience impact, and form. These questions became more useful than purely positive responses because they showed me where the project still needed development.

The most important lesson from this week was that making is not only about producing something visible, but about exposing assumptions. My sketch assumed that a web-based map interface was the right direction, but the feedback forced me to ask whether that was the strongest form, and whether the project’s critical purpose was actually clear. By the end of the week, I had not fully solved these questions, but I had a much sharper understanding of them. This made Week 07 an important developmental stage, because it pushed the project beyond a first idea and into a more self-critical design process.
