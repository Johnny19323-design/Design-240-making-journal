# Week 9

## In-Class Activities
### 1. Project Statement: First Draft

Week 9 marks a significant shift from exploring the project primarily through sketches, prototypes and feedback to articulating the project more clearly through writing. According to the lecture slides, the focus of concept development this week is on drafting the first version of the project statement. The slides emphasise that the project statement is one of the three main assessment components of the ‘Data-Driven Visualisation’ project, the other two being the process documentation and the final deliverable. It is also made clear that this statement should serve as a concise ‘exhibition wall description’ intended for a general audience, rather than an academic paper.

The class first used a case study, Xeno Computer 0.1: Labor by Tega Brain and Sam Lavigne, to help us think critically about what a project statement should do. The questions in the slides asked us to identify the data sources in the work, the future scenario it addressed, what the statement argued about data and power, and what intended impact it seemed to have. This exercise was helpful because it showed that a strong statement is not only descriptive. It must also reveal the project’s provocation, its critical position, and the way it frames data politically and socially.

After this, the class moved into drafting with NotebookLM. The slides instructed us to compile relevant sources, including the Reflective Proposal, journal entries from Weeks 7 and 8, visual references, and data source documentation, and then use these materials to generate a first draft. The class also provided a prompt structure reminding us that the statement should cover what the visualisation is, which data source is used, what subject matter and future scenario it addresses, how it engages critically with data representation, and what impact it hopes to have on a public audience. The grading rubric shown in class also made the expectations very clear: for a strong result, the statement needed to be concise, comprehensive, and articulate the work’s critical engagement and intended impact effectively.

For my own project, this drafting process proved particularly helpful, as it forced me to define the true core of the work more clearly. Prior to this, I had been circling around concepts such as ‘public nature’, ‘data systems’, ‘classification’ and ‘campsite access’, but the process of writing the project statement allowed me to synthesise these ideas into a more coherent argument. I had to move beyond descriptions of interfaces or datasets and instead articulate the core message the work seeks to convey. This shifted the project’s focus from a generalised visualisation of environmental data to a specific exploration of how public natural spaces are organised and experienced through data.

The subsequent evaluation phase was equally crucial. The class slides prompted us to consider: which parts work well? Which seem underdeveloped? Which formulations were too general or smacked of ‘artificial intelligence’? And which aspects still required further research? This prompted me to examine my draft with a critical eye, rather than treating it as a final version. I thus gained a clearer awareness of which parts of the project proposal might still appear broad or vague, particularly regarding the anticipated impact. It also led me to write a sentence that clearly committed to the project’s direction, which was a crucial step in narrowing the scope of the research.

### 2. Making Sprint

The second major in-class activity was the making sprint. The slides described rapid prototyping as a time-boxed and focused way of making, based on short cycles of iteration and an experimental mindset, where the goal is to create something tangible and testable rather than polished. Before beginning the sprint itself, the class was asked to spend ten minutes planning. This included deciding what the project most needed at that stage, what exactly would be tested or developed, what information would be gathered through making, and what tools or materials would be needed.

For me, this planning stage was valuable because it connected the project statement directly to the act of making. Instead of treating writing and prototyping as separate tasks, Week 9 made them reinforce each other. The question became: if my statement argued that the project is about how public nature is organised through data, then what kind of prototype best tests or embodies that claim? This encouraged me to focus my sprint not only on interface appearance, but on the clarity of the system itself. The project most needed a more focused and testable form, something that could demonstrate how filters, categories, and mapped points produce a structured experience of environmental place.

The sprint itself therefore became an exercise in committing to a direction. Rather than continuing to keep all options equally open, I used the sprint to make focused progress toward a more resolved screen-based path. The slides explicitly framed this as a development week where students should commit to the form of the final artefact and work toward specific goals. That was significant for my project, because earlier weeks had still left open the possibility of screen-based versus physical outcomes. By Week 9, even if the final showcase form was not fully decided, the working prototype clearly needed to move forward in one direction so that I could properly test the interface, the data translation, and the experience of interaction.

### 3. Round Robin Rapid Reactions

The final classroom activity was the ‘rapid feedback loop’. The class was divided into presenters and visitors: the presenters displayed their draft project proposals and sprint outcomes at fixed stations, whilst the visitors moved between the stations to offer feedback. Visitors’ questions included: whether the project proposal clearly articulated the core proposition; what insights the sprint deliverables offered regarding the direction of the work; what thoughts or feelings the work had evoked in them; and what issues still needed to be resolved before the Week 12 showcase. Presenters were required to record the questions, feedback and ideas they received in their journals.

This exercise is extremely useful because it tests not only the project itself, but also whether the project statement and the prototype complement one another. If the statement is clear and precise but the prototype fails to reflect this, this disconnect becomes apparent. Similarly, if the prototype is visually engaging but the statement fails to articulate the ideas it evokes, this too is laid bare. For my project, this exercise reinforced the importance of maintaining consistency between the conceptual framework and the form. I have become increasingly aware that the final piece must embody the critical concepts described in the project statement. The lecture slides also defined ‘critical’ as encompassing concepts such as urgency, care, discernment, re-examination, challenge and the provision of new perspectives. This made me realise that the project requires not only the systematic organisation of information about the campsite, but also the provocation of a shift in how people understand environmental data.

## Independent Study
### 1. Project Development

The first independent study task for Week 9 was to continue project development based directly on the making sprint and the reactions received during the round robin exchange. The class overview specifically asked us to refine the project statement alongside our making and to document both textual and visual evidence of progress.

In this phase, I focused on tightening the relationship between concept and prototype. Earlier weeks had helped me gather references, test formats, and identify feedback themes, but Week 9 made it necessary to move toward a more integrated direction. The project statement had clarified that the work was not simply about campsites as destinations, but about how public nature is structured through categories such as access, facilities, capacity, and policy. This meant that the prototype also needed to foreground those organisational systems rather than functioning as a neutral browsing interface.

This stage of development was therefore less about adding many new features and more about strengthening alignment. The project needed to become more legible as a critical design proposition. The class examples and rubric reminded me that a successful outcome should not only show data clearly, but communicate why the work matters and what kind of shift in perspective it hopes to create. That pushed me to keep asking whether each design decision supported the statement, or whether it only made the interface more functional.

### 2. Progress Report Preparation

The second independent study task was to prepare another short five-minute progress report for the following week. This slideshow needed to cover where the project currently stood, the current draft project statement, key developments and decisions since Week 8, visual research or references, and two or three specific questions for feedback.

Preparing this next presentation was important because it created another moment of consolidation. By Week 9, the project was no longer at the stage of broad possibility. It now needed to present a more committed direction, a more concrete prototype, and a more precise statement of intent. This requirement pushed me to think not only about what had changed since Week 8, but also about what still remained unresolved before the next critique and ultimately before the public showcase in Week 12.

## Reflection

Week 9 was significant because it brought conceptual writing and practical making together much more directly than before. Earlier weeks had helped me define the dataset, collect references, and respond to feedback, but this week required me to express the project as a coherent proposition in both words and form. Writing the first draft of the project statement was especially valuable because it forced me to clarify not just what the project looked like, but what it was actually trying to argue.

The making sprint and round robin reactions then tested whether that argument could be embodied in a prototype. This made Week 9 feel like a point of commitment. The project could no longer remain broadly exploratory. It had to begin resolving into a clearer final direction. The most important lesson from this week was that a strong data-driven project is not defined only by the quality of its visuals or interface, but by the coherence between data source, critical framing, future scenario, and intended public impact. That coherence became the main priority going forward.
