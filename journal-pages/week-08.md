# Week 8

## In-Class Activities
### 1. Progress Reports 

Week 8 centred on critique, presentation, and direction-setting. According to the class slides, the main in-class activity was the five-minute progress report, followed by structured group feedback. The learning outcomes for the week emphasised being able to articulate key project decisions, respond to conceptual and technical critique, and identify clear priorities for further development. The class format also introduced specific feedback roles, including Chair, Technical Commenter, and Conceptual Commenter, which reinforced the idea that project feedback should address both practical and critical dimensions of the work.

![Class Image](../assets/week-08/design240(p2).png)
*Week 8 Slide - https://canva.link/7iei6uccd3qb0j7*

For this week, I presented a five-slide progress report about my DOC Campsites project. In the slideshow, I framed the project around the question of how public nature is organised through data, using the DOC Campsites dataset as the main source. The presentation explained that my chosen data source was DOC Campsites from the New Zealand open data catalogue, that my current direction was a screen-based interactive visualisation rather than a normal tourism map, and that the critical focus was on showing how environmental places are sorted by access, capacity, facilities, and policy. The slides also showed that my concept sketch had already developed a map + filter + detail-panel structure, and that peer critique had pushed me to think more seriously about whether the final outcome should remain screen-based or expand into a physical or spatial form.

Presenting the project in slideshow form was useful because it forced me to simplify the project into a few key statements. Compared with earlier weeks, I had to explain not only what the project looked like, but what had changed and what questions still remained unresolved. The most important shift I articulated in the slides was that the project needed a stronger unique point. Rather than asking only “where are campsites?”, the work needed to ask “how does data define nature?” This became a crucial phrase for the week because it marked a clearer move away from an informational interface and toward a more critical design proposition.

Following the presentation, I received a wide range of highly valuable feedback. Several comments questioned whether the project was still too closely aligned with an app or information tool, asking why the world needs this project and how it would transform the way we experience places. Some comments enquired about the data sources and suggested that these be presented more clearly within the work. Others proposed adding more interactive features, such as allowing users to upload content to a blank map, creating two versions of the map, or considering the integration of live imagery or real-time data. There were also some more practical suggestions, such as adding zoom buttons, focusing on a specific region of New Zealand rather than the whole country, using different shapes for markers to avoid relying solely on colour, and considering how to present popularity or user experience more clearly. This feedback directly addressed the type of feedback the course encouraged speakers to seek, as it touched upon both conceptual objectives and technical form.

The most significant aspect of this feedback lies not in the individual suggestions themselves, but in the recurring patterns behind them. The same issue kept emerging: the project must not remain merely a tool for presenting information; it needs to demonstrate how the use of data can transform people’s relationship with places. This echoes concerns raised in Week 7, but by Week 8, these concerns had become more specific and urgent.

### 2. Critical Design Propositions

The second major classroom activity was the ‘Critical Design Proposal’ exercise. The course slides described this as an exchange with a partner from another group: each person would first briefly present their own project and feedback to the other, and then propose specific alternatives or extensions to their partner’s work. The aim of this exercise was not merely to offer criticism, but to transform that criticism into concrete design directions that the partner had not yet explored.

The significance of this activity lies in its transformation of criticism from description into proposal. I am no longer merely listening to others point out what is lacking in my work; instead, I must consider how to adjust, strengthen or reconfigure another project. At the same time, I receive proposals from my partner, which helps me examine my own project from a different perspective. This exercise encourages me to think more proactively: what constitutes a design decision? A project can be transformed not only through refinement, but also through conceptual repositioning.

In relation to my own project, the critical design proposition process reinforced the idea that form and concept were still entangled. The possibility of a physical or tactile extension, which I had already included as one of two directions in my progress report, became more plausible during this week. My slideshow explicitly compared a screen-based interface with a physical or tactile extension, suggesting that a material map could make access, density, and facility differences feel more embodied in a public showcase. This comparison helped me understand that the question of form was not just aesthetic. It was directly related to how the project’s critical message might be experienced.

## Independent Study
### 1. Reflective Summary

The first independent study task for Week 8 was to write a short reflective summary based on the feedback from the progress report and the critical design proposition exercise. The weekly overview emphasised that this summary should not simply list everything that was said, but should identify the most significant feedback, explain the decisions being made in response, and clarify how those decisions would shape the project going forward.

Reflecting on the week, the most significant feedback was the repeated challenge to make the project more distinct from an existing campsite information tool. While earlier feedback had already hinted at this issue, Week 8 made it much more explicit. The question “why does the world need this?” was especially important because it forced me to confront the project’s relevance beyond functional usability. If the project simply showed campsites clearly, that was not enough. It needed to alter how users think about environmental space and about the systems that mediate access to it.

A second important point was the tension between screen-based clarity and richer forms of interaction. Some of the feedback suggested more interactivity, such as user uploads or dual map modes, while other suggestions focused on improving usability through zoom, localising the map scale, or varying marker shapes. These comments helped me recognise that the project would need to balance conceptual ambition with clarity. At this stage, not every suggestion needed to be followed literally. The more important task was identifying which comments pointed to the deeper issue: how to move from displaying information to staging a different experience of data.

### 2. Project Development

The second independent study task was project development. Building on the reflective summary, I needed to decide which feedback to carry forward and how that would affect the project’s next steps.

The clearest decision I have made is to continue using screen prototypes in the next phase, rather than switching entirely to physical prototypes. This decision is consistent with the strategy outlined in the progress report: to start with a simple digital prototype and then decide whether the final form should remain as a screen prototype or evolve into a physical prototype. This approach is pragmatic, as it allows me to test interactions, navigation, visibility and layout before making any major decisions regarding the physical form.

At the same time, I did not ignore the physical direction. Instead, I treated it as an important speculative option that continued to shape how I thought about the project’s public experience. This was especially useful because the project was heading toward a public showcase in Week 12, where embodiment and display would matter. The key point was that Week 8 helped me stop thinking about the project as a neutral interface and start thinking more deliberately about user experience, public encounter, and interpretive impact.

Another development at this stage was that I became more aware of the importance of showing where the data comes from and how it structures the experience. The feedback made clear that users should not only interact with the dataset, but also understand that the dataset itself is part of the project’s meaning. This reinforced my existing critical framing and suggested that future development should make the dataset’s source, categories, and organisational logic more explicit in the design.

## Reflection

Week 8 marked a significant turning point, as it compelled me to publicly test the project, receive structured feedback, and make decisions based on that input. The progress report not only summarised the work done so far, but also revealed whether the project’s direction was clear and whether the questions I had raised were sufficiently compelling. The feedback indicated that the project already had a clear visual direction, but that its conceptual uniqueness still needed to be strengthened.

My most profound realisation this week was that criticism is only truly valuable when it helps to prioritise. The most helpful comments were not necessarily the most detailed technical suggestions, but rather those that highlighted gaps in the project’s conceptual foundation. By the end of Week 8, I had come to realise very clearly that the project must move beyond merely ‘displaying campsite data’ and instead construct a more concrete experience that demonstrates how environmental spaces are shaped, organised and understood through data systems. Consequently, this week marked a turning point where the project’s future direction began to crystallise, gradually distilling into a more focused design proposition.
